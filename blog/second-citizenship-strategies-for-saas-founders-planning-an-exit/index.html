Second Citizenship Strategies for SaaS Founders Planning an Exit: A 2026 Playbook

November 16, 2026 | Meridian Advisory

By Meridian Advisory | Updated June 2026

You've spent years building your SaaS company. The ARR is strong, the due diligence folders are organized, and conversations with acquirers or PE firms are getting serious. You're thinking about deal structure, earn-outs, and tax implications.

But here's the question almost no founder asks early enough:

Where will you be a tax resident when the liquidity event hits?

For SaaS founders staring down a seven-, eight-, or nine-figure exit, the difference between planning your citizenship strategy 18 months before close versus 18 days before close can be worth millions — sometimes tens of millions — in after-tax proceeds.

This isn't about loopholes. It's about leverage, optionality, and the kind of global structuring that sophisticated founders are increasingly treating as a standard part of exit planning.

Let's break it down.

Why SaaS Exits and CBI Are a Natural Fit

SaaS businesses are, by design, location-independent. Your servers are in AWS regions. Your team is distributed. Your customers pay via Stripe from 40 countries. There's no factory floor tying you to a single jurisdiction.

And yet, when the exit happens, the tax treatment of your capital gains is determined almost entirely by where you live — or more precisely, where you hold tax residency and citizenship.

Consider the contrast:

| Jurisdiction | Capital Gains Tax on Exit Proceeds |

|---|---|

| United States | Up to 37% (federal + state, depending on structure) |

| United Kingdom | Up to 24% (with Business Asset Disposal Relief capped at £1M) |

| Canada | Up to 26.76% (with recent 2025 inclusion rate changes) |

| Portugal (NHR 2.0 / former NHR) | Potentially 0% on foreign-sourced gains with proper structuring |

| St. Kitts & Nevis | 0% capital gains tax |

| Malta | 0% on capital gains from the sale of non-Malta assets (with proper residency) |

| UAE (Dubai) | 0% personal capital gains tax |

The math speaks for itself. On a $20 million exit, the difference between a 37% tax jurisdiction and a 0% jurisdiction is $7.4 million.

That's not optimization at the margins. That's a life-changing delta.

The Timeline Problem: Why You Need to Start Now

Here's where most founders get this wrong. They think about citizenship and residency after the LOI is signed. By then, the window has often closed.

Why? Because tax authorities in high-tax jurisdictions have anti-avoidance rules that scrutinize the timing of residency changes. If you establish new residency in a zero-tax jurisdiction three months before closing a deal that was already in negotiation, you're inviting an audit — and potentially losing the benefit entirely.

The general rule of thumb:

> Start your citizenship and residency planning at least 12–24 months before a projected exit.

This gives you time to:

  • Obtain a second citizenship or permanent residency
  • Establish genuine ties to the new jurisdiction (housing, banking, social presence)
  • Sever or restructure ties to your current tax jurisdiction
  • Ensure the transition meets the "substance" requirements that tax authorities look for
  • Work with international tax counsel to align your corporate structure

If your SaaS company is growing at 50%+ YoY and you're fielding inbound interest from acquirers, the clock is already ticking.

The Four CBI Strategies SaaS Founders Are Using in 2026

1. St. Kitts & Nevis: The Speed Play

Best for: Founders who need a second passport fast and want a zero-tax domicile as a fallback.

St. Kitts & Nevis remains the gold standard of Caribbean CBI programs. Processing times in 2026 are running at approximately 60–90 days for the accelerated application process. The minimum investment is $250,000 via the Sustainable Island State Contribution (SISC) fund for a single applicant.

Why SaaS founders like it:

  • No capital gains tax, no income tax, no wealth tax
  • Visa-free or visa-on-arrival access to 150+ countries, including the EU Schengen area and the UK
  • No physical residency requirement to maintain citizenship
  • Straightforward process with a well-established legal framework

The play: Obtain St. Kitts citizenship while still resident in your current jurisdiction. When the exit timeline firms up, work with tax counsel to execute a clean residency transition. The passport gives you the option — which is exactly what exit planning is about.

2. Portugal: The Substance Strategy

Best for: Founders who want a European base, are comfortable relocating, and want to build genuine long-term residency in the EU.

Portugal's appeal for tech founders has evolved significantly. While the original Non-Habitual Residency (NHR) regime closed to new applicants in 2024, the successor framework — often referred to as NHR 2.0 or the Tax Incentive for Scientific Research and Innovation — offers a 20% flat tax rate on qualifying employment and self-employment income for eligible professionals, including tech founders and researchers.

For exit planning, the real play is establishing Portuguese tax residency and structuring the exit so that capital gains on the sale of a non-Portuguese company may qualify for favorable treatment under applicable double tax treaties.

Why SaaS founders like it:

  • EU residency and a path to Portuguese (and therefore EU) citizenship after 5 years
  • Lisbon and Porto have thriving tech ecosystems — genuine lifestyle appeal
  • Access to the entire EU single market
  • Portugal's Golden Visa program (restructured to exclude real estate in Lisbon and Porto but still available through fund subscriptions starting at €500,000) provides a residency pathway without requiring full relocation upfront

The play: Obtain a Golden Visa, begin building substance in Portugal, transition tax residency 12–18 months before exit, and work with Portuguese and home-country tax advisors to structure the sale.

3. Malta: The EU Passport Powerhouse

Best for: Founders who want an actual EU passport (not just residency) and have a higher budget.

Malta's Exceptional Investor Naturalization (MEIN) program grants Maltese — and therefore EU — citizenship. This is the only currently operational program in Europe that provides a direct path to EU citizenship through investment.

The investment requirement is significant: a combination of a €600,000+ contribution (or €750,000 via the 12-month track), a property purchase or rental, and a philanthropic donation. Total outlay for a family can exceed €1 million.

But what you get is unmatched: an EU passport, the right to live and work anywhere in the 27 EU member states, and access to Malta's favorable tax framework — where capital gains on the sale of non-Maltese assets are generally not taxed for residents who are not domiciled in Malta.

Why SaaS founders like it:

  • Full EU citizenship — the single most powerful second passport for global mobility
  • Malta's "remittance basis" tax system means foreign capital gains not remitted to Malta are not taxed
  • Well-established fintech and iGaming regulatory environment — Malta "gets" tech
  • English-speaking, stable, EU/Eurozone member

The play: Apply 14–36 months before a projected exit. Establish Maltese residency and non-domiciled status. Structure the exit so proceeds are received and held outside Malta. The tax outcome can be transformative.

4. The Dual Strategy: Caribbean Passport + UAE Residency

Best for: Founders who want maximum tax efficiency, don't need an EU base, and are comfortable with a Dubai lifestyle.

This is arguably the most popular strategy among crypto-native SaaS founders and those in the Web3 space in 2026.

How it works:

1. Obtain a St. Kitts or Grenada passport (60–120 days) for global mobility and a second nationality

2. Establish UAE tax residency in Dubai or Abu Dhabi, which has zero personal income tax and zero capital gains tax

3. Sever tax residency in your current jurisdiction using the second passport as your "anchor" nationality

4. Close the exit as a UAE tax resident

Why it works so well:

  • The UAE has signed double tax treaties with 100+ countries, adding legitimacy to the structure
  • Dubai offers genuine lifestyle substance — real office space, a booming tech ecosystem, world-class infrastructure
  • The Caribbean passport ensures you're never reliant on a single jurisdiction
  • Grenada's passport additionally offers access to the US E-2 Treaty Investor Visa, which is uniquely valuable if you want to maintain a presence in the US post-exit

The play: Begin the CBI application and UAE company formation simultaneously. Relocate to Dubai, establish genuine substance (lease, bank accounts, local directorships), and execute the exit from a zero-tax base.

The Grenada Edge: Why It Deserves a Special Mention

If there's any chance you want to re-enter the US market after your exit — whether to start a new company, invest, or simply live part-time in the US — Grenada deserves a hard look.

Grenada is one of the few CBI countries that maintains an E-2 Treaty with the United States. This means Grenadian citizens can apply for a US E-2 Investor Visa, which allows you to live and work in the US based on a qualifying investment in a US business.

For a SaaS founder who exits, takes proceeds in a tax-efficient jurisdiction, and then wants to return to Silicon Valley or Miami to build the next thing? The Grenada → E-2 pathway is the cleanest route available.

Grenada CBI key facts in 2026:

  • Minimum investment: $235,000 via the National Transformation Fund (single applicant)
  • Processing time: approximately 90–120 days
  • Visa-free access to 145+ countries, including China, the UK, and the Schengen area
  • Zero capital gains tax, zero income tax

What SaaS Founders Get Wrong About CBI and Exit Planning

Mistake #1: Treating it as a tax trick, not a structural decision

This isn't about "hiding money." It's about aligning your personal jurisdiction with the global nature of your business. Tax authorities respect genuine relocations. They penalize paper exercises.

Mistake #2: Not involving international tax counsel early enough

Your startup lawyer and your Series B tax advisor are probably not equipped to handle cross-border residency transitions. You need specialists in international tax, ideally working alongside a CBI advisory firm that understands the immigration side.

Mistake #3: Ignoring substance requirements

Buying a passport is the easy part. Building genuine connections to your new jurisdiction — a home, a bank account, a gym membership, local friends, a flight history showing regular presence — is what actually holds up under scrutiny.

Mistake #4: Assuming US citizenship limits your options

If you're a US citizen, you're taxed on worldwide income regardless of where you live. But that doesn't mean a second citizenship is useless — far from it. It provides optionality for future generations, visa-free travel, and an emergency backup. And for the small number of founders who choose to renounce US citizenship (a serious, irreversible decision), a second citizenship is a prerequisite.

Mistake #5: Waiting for the LOI

If you're reading this and you've already signed an LOI, you're late — but not necessarily too late. The specifics matter. Book a call and let's assess your timeline.

The Real ROI of Planning Ahead

Let's run a simple scenario:

Founder profile: SaaS company with $5M ARR, growing 60% YoY. Realistic exit valuation: $30–50M within 18–24 months. Currently a US tax resident in California.

Without planning:

  • Federal capital gains tax: ~20%
  • Net Investment Income Tax: 3.8%
  • California state tax: ~13.3%
  • Effective rate: ~37%
  • Tax on a $40M exit: ~$14.8M

With an 18-month Grenada CBI + UAE residency strategy (and proper US tax/renunciation counsel):

  • Capital gains tax in UAE: 0%
  • Exit tax on renunciation (if applicable): calculated on deemed disposition at time of expatriation
  • Even after exit tax and advisory fees, the net savings could be $5M–$10M+ depending on the structure

That's not theoretical. That's the kind of math that funds your next company, secures your family's future, or lets you spend the rest of your life doing exactly what you want.

(Note: US expatriation involves complex tax consequences including potential exit tax under IRC §877A. This is not a decision to take lightly and requires specialized legal and tax counsel. The above is illustrative, not advice.)
