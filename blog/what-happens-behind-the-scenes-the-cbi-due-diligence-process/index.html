What Happens Behind the Scenes: The CBI Due Diligence Process Explained

October 27, 2026 | Meridian Advisory

And how to make sure you pass with confidence.

Published by Meridian Advisory | 2026

If you're considering citizenship by investment, there's one part of the process that generates more anxiety than any other: due diligence.

It's the stage where governments decide whether you're the kind of person they want to welcome as a citizen. And in 2026, with programs under increasing international scrutiny, the bar has never been higher.

That's actually good news. Rigorous due diligence protects the integrity of the programs — and the value of the passport you're investing in.

But it does mean you need to understand what's coming and prepare accordingly.

Here's exactly what gets checked, how the process works, and what you can do right now to set yourself up for a smooth approval.

Why Due Diligence Has Gotten More Rigorous

A decade ago, some CBI programs faced criticism for lax vetting. Those days are long gone.

Today, the leading programs — St. Kitts & Nevis, Grenada, Malta, and others — employ multi-layered due diligence frameworks that rival what you'd encounter opening a private bank account in Switzerland. In many cases, they exceed them.

Several forces are driving this:

  • International pressure from the EU, OECD, and FATF to ensure CBI programs aren't used for money laundering or sanctions evasion
  • Program reputation — countries understand that a trusted passport is their most valuable export
  • Competition — programs that maintain the highest standards attract the highest-caliber applicants, which reinforces the cycle

St. Kitts & Nevis, for example, now operates under enhanced due diligence guidelines that include real-time screening against over 1,200 global watchlists. Malta's process is widely considered the most exhaustive in the industry, often taking 12–14 months.

The bottom line: due diligence isn't a rubber stamp. It's a genuine investigation. And that should give you confidence in the passport you're acquiring.

The Seven Pillars of CBI Due Diligence

While every program has its own specifics, most conduct due diligence across these core areas:

1. Identity Verification

This is foundational. Governments will verify that you are who you say you are.

What gets checked:

  • Passport validity and history (all passports, current and expired)
  • Birth certificates and national ID documents
  • Biometric data where applicable
  • Any history of name changes

Why it matters: Fraudulent identity documentation is an automatic rejection. Inconsistencies between documents — even innocent ones — create red flags that slow the process.

2. Criminal Background Check

Every reputable program conducts a global criminal records check, not just in your country of residence.

What gets checked:

  • Criminal records in your country of citizenship, residence, and any country where you've lived for an extended period
  • Interpol databases
  • International court records
  • Pending charges or ongoing investigations (not just convictions)

What you should know: A minor traffic offense won't sink your application. A fraud conviction almost certainly will. The gray area — things like dismissed charges, expunged records, or civil disputes that had criminal elements — is where expert guidance becomes critical.

3. Financial Background and Source of Funds

This is where the process gets thorough. Governments want to know two things: where your money came from and whether it was earned legitimately.

What gets checked:

  • Bank statements (typically 2–5 years)
  • Tax returns and tax compliance records
  • Source of wealth documentation (business ownership records, employment history, investment portfolios, inheritance documentation)
  • Proof that the specific funds being used for the investment have a clear, traceable origin

The standard you're measured against: The question isn't just "is this person wealthy?" It's "can this person demonstrate a clear, legitimate path from income generation to the funds being invested?" Programs are looking for a coherent financial narrative.

4. Political Exposure Screening (PEP Checks)

Politically Exposed Persons — current or former government officials, senior executives of state-owned enterprises, and their close family members — undergo heightened scrutiny.

What gets checked:

  • Current or former political roles
  • Government contracts or connections
  • Family members' political involvement
  • Beneficial ownership of entities connected to government business

Important nuance: Being a PEP doesn't automatically disqualify you. It does mean the investigation will be significantly more thorough, and you'll likely need to provide additional documentation. Some programs handle PEP applications more comfortably than others — this is an area where choosing the right program matters enormously.

5. Sanctions and Watchlist Screening

Your name, aliases, and associated entities will be run against a comprehensive set of global databases.

What gets checked:

  • OFAC (U.S. Office of Foreign Assets Control) sanctions list
  • EU consolidated sanctions list
  • UN Security Council sanctions
  • FATF blacklists and grey lists
  • Country-specific watchlists
  • Adverse media screening (global news databases)

The media component matters more than people realize. If your name has appeared in connection with fraud allegations, regulatory actions, or controversial business dealings — even in local-language media — there's a strong chance it will surface. Programs increasingly use AI-driven media monitoring tools that scan coverage in dozens of languages.

6. Business and Corporate Due Diligence

If your wealth derives from business ownership, expect your companies to be investigated too.

What gets checked:

  • Corporate registration and ownership structure
  • Beneficial ownership chains (they will look through holding companies and trusts)
  • Industry and sector (certain industries trigger enhanced scrutiny — gambling, cryptocurrency, arms, extractive industries)
  • Business litigation history
  • Regulatory compliance record
  • Relationships with sanctioned countries or entities

For crypto investors and founders: The industry has matured significantly, but you should be prepared to provide extra documentation — exchange records, wallet histories, audit reports. Programs are becoming more comfortable with crypto-derived wealth, but the documentation burden is higher.

7. Personal Background and Character Assessment

This is the most subjective element, and it's often underestimated.

What gets checked:

  • Immigration history (visa refusals, deportations, overstays)
  • Civil litigation history
  • Professional regulatory actions (disbarment, license revocations, securities violations)
  • Associations with individuals who are sanctioned or under investigation
  • General reputation within your business community

A note on visa refusals: Having been denied a visa — even a U.S. or Schengen visa — doesn't necessarily disqualify you. But you need to be transparent about it and prepared to explain the circumstances. What will disqualify you is failing to disclose it when the due diligence team finds it independently.

Who Actually Conducts the Investigation?

Most people assume it's just the government processing your application. In reality, the due diligence ecosystem involves multiple parties:

  • Government agencies — the citizenship unit of the relevant country reviews the final file
  • Third-party due diligence firms — most programs contract with internationally recognized firms. Names you'll encounter include Thomson Reuters World-Check, Exiger, S-RM, and others. These firms have investigators on the ground in major jurisdictions worldwide.
  • Your authorized agent — reputable firms like Meridian Advisory conduct their own preliminary due diligence before submission, identifying and addressing potential issues before they reach government reviewers

This layered approach means your application is essentially being investigated three times. Nothing of substance goes unnoticed.

How to Prepare: A Practical Checklist

Now that you know what's being examined, here's how to prepare proactively:

✅ Start Gathering Documents Early

Don't wait until you've chosen a program. Begin compiling:

  • [ ] All current and expired passports
  • [ ] Birth certificates (apostilled)
  • [ ] Marriage/divorce certificates if applicable
  • [ ] Police clearance certificates from every country you've lived in (these often take 4–8 weeks to obtain)
  • [ ] Tax returns for the last 5 years
  • [ ] Bank statements for the last 2–5 years
  • [ ] Corporate registration documents for all businesses
  • [ ] Professional reference letters (2–3 from established business contacts)

✅ Create a Source of Funds Narrative

Before you're asked, write a clear, chronological explanation of how you built your wealth. Think of it as your financial biography:

> "I founded [Company] in 2014. The company grew to $X revenue by 2020. I sold my equity stake for $X in 2022. Those funds were deposited into [Bank] and subsequently invested in [assets]. The funds for this application come from [specific account/source]."

Every claim should be supported by documentation.

✅ Conduct a Self-Audit

Google yourself. In English and in your native language. Check:

  • Are there any negative media mentions?
  • Are there any court records associated with your name?
  • Are there any other individuals with your name who have adverse records? (You'd be surprised how often this creates complications.)

If you find something concerning, don't hide it. Bring it to your advisor's attention immediately. Disclosed issues are manageable. Discovered issues are problematic.

✅ Resolve Outstanding Issues

If you have:

  • Pending tax disputes — work toward resolution or get documentation showing compliance
  • Unresolved civil litigation — get a status letter from your attorney
  • Expired identification documents — renew them now
  • Inconsistencies between documents (different name spellings, different date formats) — get official clarification letters

✅ Be Prepared to Explain Complexity

If your financial life involves multiple jurisdictions, holding companies, trusts, or crypto assets — that's perfectly fine. Many of the world's most successful people have complex structures. But be ready to explain why each structure exists and provide documentation for each entity in the chain.

Complexity isn't a problem. Unexplained complexity is.

Common Reasons Applications Get Denied

Understanding why others fail helps you avoid the same fate:

| Reason | Frequency | Preventable? |

|---|---|---|

| Undisclosed criminal history | High | Yes — always disclose |

| Sanctions or watchlist match | Moderate | Depends on circumstances |

| Insufficient source of funds documentation | High | Yes — prepare thoroughly |

| Adverse media coverage | Moderate | Partially — context matters |

| Visa refusal history (undisclosed) | Moderate | Yes — always disclose |

| False or fraudulent documents | High | Obviously — never do this |

| PEP status (unmanaged) | Low-Moderate | Yes — choose the right program |

The single most common thread: lack of transparency. Programs are far more forgiving of complicated backgrounds than they are of applicants who try to hide things.

How Long Does Due Diligence Take?

It varies significantly by program:

  • St. Kitts & Nevis: 2–4 months (accelerated processing available)
  • Grenada: 3–4 months
  • Portugal Golden Visa: 6–12+ months (includes broader immigration processing)
  • Malta: 12–14 months (the most thorough process in the industry)

These timelines are for straightforward applications. If your case involves PEP status, complex corporate structures, or connections to higher-risk jurisdictions, add additional time.
