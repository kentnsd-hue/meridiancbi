Why Family Offices Are Adding Second Citizenships to Their Wealth Planning Toolkit

November 04, 2026 | Meridian Advisory

Published by Meridian Advisory | June 2026

The modern family office operates in a world that looks nothing like it did a decade ago. Geopolitical fragmentation, shifting tax regimes, capital controls, and an increasingly unpredictable regulatory landscape have forced even the most conservative wealth stewards to rethink what "comprehensive planning" actually means.

In 2026, one strategy has moved decisively from the periphery to the mainstream: Citizenship by Investment (CBI).

What was once dismissed as a niche maneuver for the ultra-mobile is now showing up in the strategic plans of single-family offices, multi-family offices, and the advisors who serve them. Here's why — and what's driving the shift.

1. Jurisdictional Diversification Is No Longer Optional

Family offices have long understood the principle of diversification across asset classes. But the events of the past several years — from banking crises and sanctions regimes to sudden changes in residency-based taxation — have made one thing painfully clear:

Concentrating your family's entire legal identity in a single jurisdiction is itself a risk.

A second citizenship provides what no investment portfolio can: optionality at the sovereign level. It's the ability to bank, travel, reside, and structure holdings across multiple legal frameworks — not as a reaction to crisis, but as a proactive layer of resilience.

Leading family office networks, including those surveyed in the 2025–2026 UBS Global Family Office Report, have increasingly cited "geopolitical hedging" as a top-three strategic priority. Second citizenship is one of the most direct ways to act on that priority.

2. The Tax Planning Landscape Demands Flexibility

The global tax environment in 2026 is defined by complexity and motion:

  • The OECD's global minimum tax framework (Pillar Two) continues to reshape where and how wealth is held.
  • The EU's evolving tax transparency directives are creating new reporting obligations for residents of member states.
  • Countries across Asia, Latin America, and the Middle East are revising their tax residency rules, sometimes with minimal notice.

For family offices managing multi-generational wealth, a second citizenship — particularly in a jurisdiction with favorable tax treaty networks, no wealth tax, or territorial taxation — provides strategic breathing room. It doesn't replace tax planning; it multiplies the options available to advisors structuring trusts, holding companies, and succession frameworks.

Grenada, for example, offers citizenship that provides access to the U.S. E-2 Treaty Investor Visa — a pathway that no amount of money can buy directly and one that opens significant structuring opportunities for families with U.S.-facing business interests.

3. Travel Mobility Is a Hard Asset

This point is often underestimated in family office circles because principals are accustomed to traveling freely. But consider:

  • A St. Kitts & Nevis passport provides visa-free or visa-on-arrival access to over 155 countries, including the UK, EU Schengen Area, and Singapore.
  • A Malta citizenship grants full EU rights — the ability to live, work, and move freely across 27 member states.
  • A Portugal Golden Visa pathway offers a route to EU residency and eventually citizenship, anchored by one of Europe's most stable democracies.

For families with business operations spanning multiple continents, with next-generation members studying or working abroad, or with principals who simply refuse to be constrained by visa queues and consular backlogs — passport strength is a functional asset class.

Family offices are beginning to evaluate passport portfolios the same way they evaluate real estate portfolios: by access, utility, and long-term strategic value.

4. Succession and Next-Generation Planning

Here's where CBI becomes particularly compelling for the family office context.

Most CBI programs allow the inclusion of dependent children, spouses, parents, and in some cases siblings under a single application. Several programs — St. Kitts and Grenada among them — grant citizenship that is hereditary, passing to future generations born after the principal obtains status.

This means a decision made today by the family patriarch or matriarch can provide decades of optionality for children and grandchildren who may:

  • Want to study at European universities with EU-resident tuition rates
  • Launch businesses in jurisdictions different from the family's home base
  • Marry internationally and need flexible residency options
  • Require a safe harbor during regional instability

When framed this way, CBI isn't an expense — it's an intergenerational infrastructure investment.

5. Speed and Certainty in an Uncertain World

Unlike traditional immigration pathways that can take years and involve uncertain outcomes, established CBI programs offer something rare in the legal world: a defined process with a predictable timeline.

  • St. Kitts & Nevis: Approvals typically in 60–90 days, with an accelerated option available.
  • Grenada: Processing in approximately 90–120 days.
  • Malta Exceptional Investor Naturalization: A more rigorous process, but with a clear pathway to full EU citizenship.

For family offices accustomed to executing decisions with precision and speed, the structured nature of CBI programs aligns well with how they operate. There's a clear investment threshold, a documented due diligence process, and a binary outcome. Compared to the ambiguity of many EB-5 applications or entrepreneur visa programs, the clarity is a feature, not a bug.

6. Due Diligence Standards Have Matured

One of the historical objections to CBI — that programs lacked rigor — has largely been addressed. The leading programs in 2026 operate under:

  • Multi-tier due diligence conducted by internationally recognized compliance firms
  • Memoranda of understanding with Interpol, the FBI, and other law enforcement agencies
  • Regulatory oversight that has been tightened significantly following industry reforms in 2023–2025

For family offices, which are themselves subject to increasing compliance scrutiny from banks, regulators, and counterparties, the improved due diligence infrastructure of CBI programs is actually an advantage. A citizenship obtained through a rigorous, transparent process reinforces rather than undermines the family's compliance posture.

The Strategic Framing: Citizenship as Infrastructure

The family offices that are leading on this issue aren't thinking about second citizenship as a "plan B." They're thinking about it as core infrastructure — in the same category as:

  • A properly structured family trust
  • A diversified custody arrangement across multiple banking jurisdictions
  • A robust cybersecurity and privacy framework
  • A succession plan that accounts for regulatory change

The question isn't whether a family needs a second citizenship today. The question is whether the family's wealth plan is resilient enough to absorb a shock that makes mobility, jurisdictional access, or tax flexibility suddenly urgent.

History suggests that by the time you need these things, the window to obtain them has already narrowed.
