5 Myths About Second Citizenship: Why a New Passport Doesn't Mean Giving Up Your First

October 29, 2026 | Meridian Advisory

Published by Meridian Advisory | June 2026

There's one fear that stops more people from pursuing second citizenship than cost, paperwork, or processing times combined.

It's the belief that getting a new passport means surrendering the one you already have.

This misconception is remarkably persistent — and remarkably wrong. At Meridian Advisory, it's the single most common concern we hear in initial consultations. So let's dismantle it, myth by myth, with facts.

Myth #1: "If I Get a Second Citizenship, My Home Country Will Revoke My First"

The reality: The vast majority of countries in the world permit dual (or even multiple) citizenship. The United States, the United Kingdom, Canada, Australia, France, Germany, Ireland, and dozens more all allow their citizens to hold additional nationalities without penalty.

When you acquire citizenship in St. Kitts & Nevis, Grenada, Malta, or Portugal through an investment program, you are adding a citizenship. You are not replacing anything.

According to a 2024 analysis by the Global Citizenship Observatory (GLOBALCIT), over 75% of countries worldwide now accept some form of dual nationality — a figure that has nearly doubled since the early 1990s.

The bottom line: For most passport holders, acquiring a second citizenship is a purely additive process. Nothing is taken away.

Myth #2: "The U.S. Will Tax Me More or Flag Me If I Hold a Second Passport"

The reality: The United States has no law prohibiting dual citizenship. The Supreme Court affirmed this in Afroyim v. Rusk (1967), ruling that U.S. citizenship cannot be involuntarily revoked.

As a U.S. citizen, you are already taxed on worldwide income regardless of where you live or how many passports you hold. A second citizenship doesn't change your tax obligations — it doesn't increase them, and it doesn't decrease them.

What a second citizenship does provide is optionality: the ability to reside, bank, and do business in additional jurisdictions. Whether and how you use that optionality is a separate strategic conversation — one best had with qualified tax counsel and a CBI advisor who understands the full picture.

> Important note: U.S. citizens who voluntarily renounce their citizenship face exit tax provisions under the HEART Act. But renunciation is a deliberate, affirmative choice. Acquiring a second passport does not trigger it. These are completely separate actions.

Myth #3: "Citizenship by Investment Programs Aren't 'Real' Citizenship"

The reality: Citizenship obtained through a CBI program is full, legal, constitutional citizenship in the issuing country. It is not a residency permit. It is not a visa. It is not a second-tier status.

Citizens of St. Kitts & Nevis, Grenada, and Malta who obtained their status through investment hold the exact same passport, the exact same rights, and the exact same international standing as citizens born in those countries.

As of 2026:

  • A St. Kitts & Nevis passport provides visa-free or visa-on-arrival access to 150+ destinations, including the UK, EU Schengen Zone, and Singapore.
  • A Grenada passport provides access to 145+ destinations and — critically — eligibility for the U.S. E-2 Treaty Investor Visa, a benefit unavailable to citizens of many larger nations.
  • Malta's program grants EU citizenship, meaning full rights to live, work, and travel across all 27 EU member states.

These are not consolation prizes. They are among the most strategically valuable travel documents in the world.

Myth #4: "My Employer or Business Partners Will Find Out and It Will Create Problems"

The reality: Citizenship status is not published in a public registry. There is no global database that your employer, partners, or competitors can search to discover whether you hold additional nationalities.

In most CBI programs, the process is handled with strict confidentiality. At Meridian Advisory, discretion isn't a bonus feature — it's foundational to how we operate.

Many of the high-net-worth individuals, tech founders, and investors we work with choose to keep their second citizenship entirely private. Others are open about it. The point is that it's your decision.

It's also worth noting that dual citizenship is increasingly normalized, particularly among globally mobile professionals. In 2026, holding multiple nationalities is less a curiosity and more a marker of strategic sophistication.

Myth #5: "Second Citizenship Is Only Useful If You Plan to Leave Your Country"

The reality: The majority of our clients at Meridian Advisory have no intention of leaving their home country. They aren't fleeing. They're planning.

A second citizenship is best understood as infrastructure — a foundational layer of optionality that serves you across multiple scenarios:

  • Political instability at home? You have a secure fallback jurisdiction.
  • Business expansion? You can establish a presence in new markets without visa constraints.
  • Family security? Most CBI programs extend to spouses, children, and often parents and siblings.
  • Banking and asset diversification? Multiple citizenships unlock access to financial systems in stable, well-regulated jurisdictions.
  • Travel freedom? Eliminate visa queues, reduce friction, and move through the world on your terms.

You don't buy fire insurance because you expect your house to burn down. You buy it because you understand that the cost of preparation is always lower than the cost of crisis.

The Countries That Do Restrict Dual Citizenship

In the interest of full transparency, a small number of countries do impose restrictions on dual nationality. These include:

  • China — Does not recognize dual citizenship; acquiring another nationality results in automatic loss of Chinese citizenship.
  • India — Does not permit dual citizenship, though it offers an OCI (Overseas Citizen of India) card as a partial workaround.
  • Singapore — Requires male citizens to complete National Service obligations and does not permit dual citizenship for adults.
  • UAE — Emirati citizenship law does not traditionally recognize dual nationality, though enforcement and policy are evolving.
  • Saudi Arabia — Generally does not permit dual citizenship without royal authorization.

If you hold citizenship in one of these countries, the calculus is different — but not necessarily prohibitive. The specifics matter, and that's precisely the kind of nuanced conversation our advisory team is equipped to navigate.

The Real Question Isn't Whether You'll Lose Something. It's What You Stand to Gain.

Second citizenship through investment is not about abandoning your identity, your home, or your loyalties. It's about expanding your options in a world where borders, regulations, and geopolitical realities can shift faster than any individual can predict.

The wealthiest and most strategically minded families on earth have understood this for generations. In 2026, with CBI programs that are more accessible, more rigorously regulated, and more globally recognized than ever, this kind of planning is no longer reserved for the ultra-elite.

It's available to any qualified individual who decides that preparation is worth more than hope.
