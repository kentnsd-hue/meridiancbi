The Cost of Waiting: Why CBI Program Prices Only Move in One Direction

November 02, 2026 | Meridian Advisory

Published by Meridian Advisory | June 2026

If you've been "thinking about it" for the past few years, that hesitation has already cost you six figures.

Citizenship by Investment isn't getting cheaper. It never has. And if history is any guide — and it is — every quarter you delay is a quarter where minimum investment thresholds, due diligence fees, and processing costs creep higher.

Let's look at the data.

A Decade of Price Increases: The Numbers Don't Lie

St. Kitts & Nevis

The world's oldest CBI program has been a masterclass in incremental price escalation:

  • 2015: The Sustainable Growth Fund (SGF) donation option launched at $150,000 for a single applicant.
  • 2018: Fees adjusted upward. Additional dependent costs increased across the board.
  • 2023: The minimum SGF contribution rose to $250,000 for a single applicant — a 67% increase from the original threshold.
  • 2024–2025: New due diligence requirements and third-party verification layers added further costs, pushing the all-in price for a family of four well past $350,000.
  • 2026 (current): Industry analysts anticipate another adjustment before year-end as St. Kitts continues to align with international compliance standards.

That's not inflation. That's a structural repricing of access.

Grenada

Grenada's program is uniquely valuable because it's one of the few Caribbean CBI options that includes access to the U.S. E-2 Treaty Investor Visa — a benefit no amount of money can replicate if the treaty relationship ever changes.

  • 2019: Minimum National Transformation Fund (NTF) donation was $150,000.
  • 2023: Increased to $235,000 for a single applicant.
  • 2024–2025: Additional government fees, enhanced due diligence charges, and processing costs brought the effective total meaningfully higher.

The E-2 treaty access alone makes Grenada's program worth multiples of its sticker price. But that access isn't guaranteed forever — treaty relationships are diplomatic, not contractual.

Portugal Golden Visa

Portugal's trajectory tells perhaps the most dramatic cautionary tale:

  • 2012: The program launched with a €500,000 real estate investment option. Straightforward. Accessible.
  • 2022: Real estate investments were restricted to low-density interior regions and the Azores/Madeira, effectively eliminating Lisbon and Porto — the cities most investors actually wanted.
  • 2023: The real estate pathway was eliminated entirely. The primary remaining option became fund investments starting at €500,000.
  • 2024–2026: Remaining fund-based pathways have faced increased regulatory scrutiny, longer processing times, and growing political pressure to further restrict or close the program.

Investors who acted in 2015 bought Lisbon apartments for €500,000 that are now worth over €900,000 — and got EU residency in the process. Those who waited got neither.

Malta

Malta's program — one of only a handful offering direct EU citizenship — has always been the most expensive, and it's only gotten more so:

  • 2020: The Malta Individual Investor Programme (MIIP) was replaced by the stricter Malta Exceptional Investor Naturalization (MEIN) policy.
  • 2022–2023: Minimum combined costs (contribution + property + donation) settled around €690,000–€960,000+ depending on residency duration.
  • 2024–2026: Enhanced EU-level scrutiny has further tightened approvals. Processing times have extended. The effective cost of an EU passport through Malta now comfortably exceeds €1 million for most family applicants.

The EU has made it unambiguously clear: it wants fewer CBI programs, not more. Malta's program survives because of careful political navigation — not because Brussels is enthusiastic about it.

Why Prices Only Go Up: The Five Structural Drivers

Understanding why this happens matters more than memorizing specific numbers. These aren't random price hikes. They're driven by forces that are accelerating, not slowing down.

1. International Regulatory Pressure

The OECD, EU, and FATF have spent the last five years intensifying pressure on CBI-offering nations. Every new compliance requirement — enhanced due diligence, source-of-funds verification, biometric screening, multi-agency background checks — adds direct cost. These costs get passed to applicants.

This pressure is not cyclical. It's structural. No international body has ever relaxed CBI scrutiny. The ratchet only turns one way.

2. Demand from an Expanding Global Wealthy Class

The number of individuals worldwide with a net worth above $1 million has grown by approximately 40% since 2018. Demand for geographic diversification, tax optimization, and political hedging is surging — particularly from:

  • Tech founders seeking post-exit flexibility
  • Crypto holders navigating an evolving regulatory patchwork
  • Entrepreneurs in politically volatile regions looking for a Plan B
  • Remote workers and digital nomads optimizing for lifestyle and tax efficiency

More demand against a fixed (and shrinking) supply of programs means higher prices. Economics 101.

3. Programs Are Closing, Not Opening

The trend line is unmistakable:

  • Bulgaria — CBI program shut down (2023)
  • Cyprus — CBI program shut down (2020)
  • Portugal — Real estate pathway eliminated (2023)
  • Ireland — Immigrant Investor Programme closed (2023)
  • UK — Tier 1 Investor Visa closed (2022)

Fewer options means the remaining programs carry more pricing power. St. Kitts, Grenada, and Malta don't need to compete on price when the alternatives are disappearing.

4. Currency and Inflation Dynamics

Most Caribbean CBI programs are denominated in USD or pegged currencies. For applicants earning in currencies that have weakened against the dollar — which includes much of the global economy over the past several years — the effective cost has risen even beyond the nominal price increases.

5. Political Will Is Finite

Every CBI program exists because a government decided the economic benefit outweighed the political cost. That calculation can shift overnight. Elections, scandals, international pressure, or a single high-profile bad actor can turn a stable program into a headline — and headlines lead to shutdowns or radical restructuring.

The programs that are open today are open because of conditions that exist today. Not tomorrow. Not next year.

The Hidden Cost: What You Lose While Waiting

The sticker price increase is only the most obvious cost of delay. Consider what you're forfeiting while you "think about it":

Tax Optimization

Every year without a second citizenship or residency is a year you can't legally restructure. For high earners, the annual tax delta can dwarf the CBI investment itself.

Visa-Free Travel

A St. Kitts passport currently provides visa-free or visa-on-arrival access to 150+ countries, including the UK, EU Schengen Zone, Singapore, and Hong Kong. That access has tangible monetary value in avoided visa fees, processing time, and business opportunities you can act on immediately.

Optionality

Second citizenship is the ultimate option contract. You may never need it — but if you do, it's because something has gone wrong, and by then, it's too late to apply. Geopolitical instability, regulatory crackdowns, banking restrictions, capital controls — these events don't announce themselves on a schedule.

Processing Time

Applications aren't instant. Even the fastest programs take 3–6 months. More complex cases or programs like Malta can take 12–15+ months. The clock doesn't start until you apply.

The Pattern Is Clear. The Question Is What You Do With It.

Every year for the past decade, at least one major CBI program has either:

  • Raised its minimum investment threshold
  • Added new fees or compliance requirements
  • Restricted eligible investment categories
  • Closed entirely

There is no historical precedent for a CBI program becoming cheaper or more accessible over time. Not one. Ever.

The investors who secured Portuguese Golden Visas in 2015 look like geniuses today. The families who locked in St. Kitts citizenship at $150,000 saved over $100,000 compared to today's rates. They weren't smarter — they just acted sooner.
