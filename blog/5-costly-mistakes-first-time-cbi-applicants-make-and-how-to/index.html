5 Costly Mistakes First-Time CBI Applicants Make (And How to Avoid Them)

October 11, 2026 | Meridian Advisory

Published by Meridian Advisory | 2026

Every year, thousands of high-net-worth individuals explore Citizenship by Investment as a strategy for global mobility, asset protection, and generational security. And every year, a significant number of those applicants hit avoidable roadblocks that delay their timelines, inflate their costs, or — in the worst cases — result in outright denial.

After guiding hundreds of clients through successful CBI applications, we've identified the five most common mistakes first-time applicants make. If you're considering a second passport in 2026, this is the roadmap of what not to do.

Mistake #1: Choosing a Program Based on Price Alone

It's the most tempting shortcut — and the most dangerous one.

A quick Google search will surface minimum investment thresholds for various programs. St. Kitts & Nevis, Grenada, Dominica, Vanuatu — they all have different price points. Many first-time applicants gravitate toward the cheapest option without considering the full picture.

Here's what they miss:

  • Visa-free travel access varies dramatically. Grenada's passport includes visa-free access to China and, critically, eligibility for the U.S. E-2 Treaty Investor Visa. Most other Caribbean programs don't offer that.
  • Due diligence fees, government processing fees, and legal costs can add tens of thousands of dollars on top of the headline investment number.
  • Tax implications differ by jurisdiction. A program that looks affordable upfront could create unexpected tax exposure depending on your residency structure.
  • Reputation and longevity of the program matter. A passport from a program under international scrutiny may lose visa-free agreements — effectively devaluing your investment overnight.

How to avoid it: Evaluate programs holistically. Match them to your specific goals — whether that's U.S. market access, EU residency, tax optimization, or simply a Plan B. The cheapest program is rarely the best-fit program.

Mistake #2: Underestimating the Due Diligence Process

Many applicants assume that if they have a clean criminal record and the required capital, approval is essentially guaranteed.

That assumption is wrong.

CBI programs conduct rigorous, multi-layered background checks. In 2026, due diligence standards are stricter than ever, driven by pressure from the EU, OECD, and international financial watchdogs. Programs that want to maintain their visa-free agreements — especially with the Schengen Area and the UK — have every incentive to reject borderline applicants.

What due diligence typically covers:

  • Criminal background checks across multiple jurisdictions
  • Source-of-funds verification (not just proving you have the money, but proving how you earned it)
  • Media and reputational screening
  • Sanctions and PEP (Politically Exposed Person) checks
  • Business and litigation history
  • Previous immigration applications and any past denials

Common pitfalls:

  • Applicants with complex corporate structures who can't clearly demonstrate their source of wealth
  • Individuals who have had prior visa refusals and don't disclose them (non-disclosure is treated far more seriously than the refusal itself)
  • Business owners in industries flagged as high-risk — such as cryptocurrency, gaming, or arms — who don't prepare supplementary documentation

How to avoid it: Prepare your documentation as if you're being audited, not as if you're filling out a form. Work with an advisory firm that conducts a pre-screening assessment before you submit your application. This is exactly what we do at Meridian Advisory — because a rejection doesn't just cost you time and money. It creates a record that can complicate future applications to any program.

Mistake #3: Going It Alone (or Working with the Wrong Agent)

The CBI industry has a middleman problem.

A quick search will surface hundreds of agencies offering citizenship services. Many of them are unlicensed resellers, solo operators with no direct government relationships, or marketing firms posing as immigration experts. Some charge inflated fees. Others cut corners on due diligence prep. A few are outright fraudulent.

Red flags to watch for:

  • No verifiable track record. Ask for case studies, client references, or proof of authorized agent status with the specific CBI unit you're applying to.
  • Guaranteed approval promises. No legitimate advisor can guarantee approval. Any firm that does is either lying or doesn't understand the process.
  • Pressure to commit quickly. Legitimate programs don't disappear overnight. If an agent is creating false urgency ("prices are going up next week!"), proceed with extreme caution.
  • Lack of transparency on fees. You should receive a full, itemized cost breakdown before you sign anything — including government fees, due diligence fees, legal costs, and the advisory fee itself.
  • No in-house legal or compliance team. Your application will be reviewed by government attorneys. Your advisor should have equivalent expertise preparing it.

How to avoid it: Work with a firm that is a licensed, authorized agent of the programs they represent. Ask hard questions. Verify credentials. A strong advisory relationship should feel like working with a trusted attorney — not a salesperson.

Mistake #4: Ignoring Tax and Structural Planning

A second passport is a powerful tool. But without proper planning around how you hold it, where you establish residency, and how it interacts with your existing tax obligations, it can create more problems than it solves.

Scenarios we see regularly:

  • U.S. citizens who obtain a Caribbean passport assuming it reduces their U.S. tax burden. It doesn't. The U.S. taxes its citizens on worldwide income regardless of how many passports they hold. The passport itself isn't a tax strategy — but it can be part of a broader restructuring plan if paired with proper expatriation planning.
  • EU residents who obtain a non-EU passport without understanding CRS (Common Reporting Standard) obligations. Your financial accounts may still be reported to your country of tax residency.
  • Business owners who don't consider where to incorporate or restructure their businesses post-citizenship to legally optimize their tax position.
  • Crypto investors who assume holding a Caribbean passport automatically shields digital assets from reporting requirements. In 2026, with the OECD's Crypto-Asset Reporting Framework (CARF) rolling out across jurisdictions, this assumption is increasingly dangerous.

How to avoid it: Treat your CBI application as one component of a comprehensive wealth and mobility strategy — not a standalone transaction. Coordinate with international tax counsel before you apply, not after. At Meridian Advisory, we work alongside our clients' existing legal and tax teams to ensure the citizenship strategy aligns with their broader financial architecture.

Mistake #5: Waiting for the "Perfect" Time to Apply

This is the silent killer of CBI plans.

We hear it constantly: "I'll apply once my funding round closes." "I want to wait until the political situation stabilizes." "I'll do it next year when I have more time."

Here's the reality of the CBI landscape in 2026:

  • Programs are tightening, not loosening. The EU continues to pressure member states on golden visa programs. Portugal's Golden Visa has already undergone significant restructuring. Malta's program has hard caps on approvals. Caribbean programs are raising investment thresholds and tightening due diligence in response to international pressure.
  • Processing times are lengthening. As application volumes grow and due diligence becomes more complex, the average time from application to passport has increased across nearly every program. What used to take 3–4 months now routinely takes 6–9 months, and some programs stretch well beyond that.
  • Your personal circumstances may change. A new business venture, a change in residency, a legal matter, even a shift in family status — any of these can complicate or delay an application that would have been straightforward months earlier.
  • Geopolitical risk doesn't wait. The clients who are most grateful for their second passport are invariably the ones who obtained it before they urgently needed it.

How to avoid it: Start the process now, even if you're not ready to commit to a specific program. A preliminary consultation takes 30 minutes. A pre-qualification assessment takes a few days. The information you gather today positions you to act decisively when timing is right — instead of scrambling when circumstances force your hand.

The Bottom Line

Citizenship by Investment is one of the most consequential financial and legal decisions a high-net-worth individual can make. The difference between a smooth, successful application and a costly, frustrating one almost always comes down to preparation, professional guidance, and strategic thinking.

The five mistakes above aren't theoretical — they're patterns we've observed across hundreds of real cases. Every single one of them is avoidable.
