Donation vs. Real Estate: How to Choose the Right CBI Investment Route

October 31, 2026 | Meridian Advisory

Published by Meridian Advisory | June 2026

If you're exploring citizenship by investment, you've likely encountered the same fork in the road that every applicant faces: Should you make a non-refundable donation to a government fund, or invest in approved real estate?

It's one of the most consequential decisions in the entire CBI process — and surprisingly, it's the one most applicants spend the least time analyzing.

Both routes lead to the same destination: a second passport, expanded global mobility, and the security that comes with a Plan B. But the financial mechanics, timelines, and long-term implications are fundamentally different. Choosing the wrong route doesn't disqualify you — it just costs you money you didn't need to spend.

Let's break it down.

Understanding the Two Routes

The Donation Route

Under the donation model, you make a one-time, non-refundable contribution to a government-designated fund — typically a National Transformation Fund, a Sustainable Growth Fund, or a similar sovereign development vehicle.

Key characteristics:

  • Lower upfront cost (typically $100,000–$200,000 for a single applicant, varying by program and family size)
  • Non-recoverable — this is not an investment you get back
  • Faster processing — fewer due diligence layers on the asset side
  • Simpler paperwork — no property purchase agreements, title searches, or escrow arrangements
  • No ongoing obligations — once paid, you're done

Programs with well-established donation routes include:

  • St. Kitts & Nevis — Sustainable Island State Contribution (SISC)
  • Grenada — National Transformation Fund (NTF)
  • Dominica — Economic Diversification Fund (EDF)
  • Antigua & Barbuda — National Development Fund (NDF)

The Real Estate Route

Under the real estate model, you purchase approved property — typically a resort, hotel share, condo, or residential development — at or above a government-mandated minimum threshold.

Key characteristics:

  • Higher upfront cost (typically $200,000–$400,000+, depending on the program)
  • Asset retention — you own a tangible property that can generate rental income or appreciate in value
  • Mandatory holding period — usually 5 to 7 years before you can resell
  • Additional costs — closing fees, government taxes, annual maintenance, property management, insurance
  • Potential resale value — though this is where expectations and reality often diverge

The Five Factors That Should Drive Your Decision

1. Total Cost of Ownership — Not Just the Sticker Price

This is where most applicants get tripped up.

On paper, the real estate route looks like the smarter financial play. You're "investing" $200,000 instead of "losing" $100,000 to a donation. But the true cost comparison requires a deeper look.

Real estate total cost example (illustrative):

| Cost Component | Estimate |

|---|---|

| Property purchase | $200,000 |

| Government fees & taxes | $15,000–$30,000 |

| Legal & closing costs | $5,000–$10,000 |

| Annual maintenance/HOA (5 years) | $10,000–$25,000 |

| Property management fees | $5,000–$15,000 |

| Insurance (5 years) | $3,000–$8,000 |

| Total actual outlay | $238,000–$288,000+ |

Now factor in the resale. CBI-approved properties exist in a secondary market that is, frankly, flooded with units being offloaded by applicants who've hit their holding period. Resale discounts of 20–40% below the original "approved" purchase price are not uncommon.

Net cost after resale could easily reach $140,000–$180,000+ — and that's before accounting for the opportunity cost of having $200,000+ locked up for five to seven years.

Compare that to a clean $100,000–$150,000 donation with zero ongoing costs and zero resale risk.

The donation route is often the more cost-effective option when you run the full numbers.

2. Your Investment Timeline and Liquidity Needs

Ask yourself: Can you comfortably lock away $200,000+ for five to seven years?

If you're an entrepreneur scaling a business, a crypto investor navigating volatile markets, or simply someone who values liquidity, tying up capital in Caribbean real estate may not align with your financial strategy.

The donation route frees up your capital allocation immediately. You take the hit, you move on, and your remaining capital stays deployable.

Real estate makes more sense for applicants who:

  • Already have diversified portfolios and don't need the liquidity
  • Genuinely want a vacation property or rental asset in the Caribbean
  • Have a long-term wealth preservation strategy that includes international real estate

3. Do You Actually Want the Property?

This is the question that separates smart real estate route applicants from everyone else.

If you're genuinely excited about owning a beachfront condo in Grenada that you'll visit twice a year and rent out the rest of the time — the real estate route adds lifestyle value that a donation simply can't match.

But if you're choosing real estate purely because it feels like you're "getting something back," pause. The CBI real estate market is a specialized, illiquid market. These are not conventional real estate investments. The properties are priced for CBI applicants, the buyer pool on resale is limited, and rental yields — while sometimes marketed at 3–5% — often underperform after management fees and vacancy rates.

Buy the property because you want the property. Not because you want to avoid the psychology of a "donation."

4. Processing Speed and Complexity

Donation applications are structurally simpler. There's no property transaction to coordinate, no title verification, no escrow, and no developer timelines to manage.

In programs like St. Kitts & Nevis and Dominica, donation-route applications can be processed in as little as 45–90 days under accelerated processing. Real estate applications in the same programs typically take longer due to the additional documentation layers.

If speed to passport is a priority — and in 2026, with shifting geopolitical landscapes, it increasingly is — the donation route has a meaningful advantage.

5. Family Size and Composition

For larger families, the donation route often scales more favorably.

Most programs offer tiered donation pricing — a single applicant might pay $100,000, while a family of four pays $150,000–$175,000. The per-person cost decreases as the family grows.

Real estate, by contrast, carries a fixed minimum purchase price regardless of family size. Whether you're a solo applicant or a family of six, you're still buying the same $200,000+ property — though government fees do increase per dependent.

For families of three or more, the donation route is almost always the better value proposition.

A Decision Framework

To simplify, here's a framework we use with clients at Meridian Advisory:

Choose the Donation Route if you:

  • ✅ Want the lowest total cost
  • ✅ Prioritize speed and simplicity
  • ✅ Value liquidity and capital flexibility
  • ✅ Have a larger family applying together
  • ✅ View CBI purely as a mobility and security tool

Choose the Real Estate Route if you:

  • ✅ Genuinely want international property exposure
  • ✅ Have excess capital that doesn't need to be liquid
  • ✅ Plan to use the property personally
  • ✅ Are comfortable with the holding period and resale dynamics
  • ✅ Want a tangible asset as part of a broader wealth diversification strategy

What About Programs That Only Offer One Route?

It's worth noting that not every program offers both options equally.

  • Portugal's Golden Visa (in its current 2026 iteration) is structured around investment fund subscriptions and select real estate in low-density areas — there is no simple donation path.
  • Malta's Exceptional Investor Naturalization combines a contribution, a property requirement (purchase or rental), and a philanthropic donation — it's a hybrid model with higher thresholds designed for ultra-HNW individuals.

For Caribbean programs — St. Kitts, Grenada, Dominica, Antigua — both routes are available and well-established, giving applicants genuine optionality.

The Bottom Line

There is no universally "right" answer. There is only the right answer for your financial situation, your family, and your goals.

What we see consistently at Meridian Advisory is that applicants who choose based on emotion — "I don't want to just give money away" — often end up paying more in total than those who ran the numbers dispassionately and chose the donation route.

And applicants who choose real estate for the right reasons — lifestyle, genuine portfolio diversification, personal use — tend to be the most satisfied with their decision long-term.

The key is making an informed choice, not a reactive one.
